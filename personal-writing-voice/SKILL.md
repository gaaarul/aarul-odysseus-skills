# Personal Writing Voice

A skill for reproducing my specific writing voice in new text, on any topic or format. This file is fully self-contained: it does not require any writing sample to be pasted into the conversation, and does not assume the reader has any other context. My actual voice profile, derived from real samples of my writing, is baked in below. Everything needed to write as me is in this file.

**Hard formatting rule, applies to every single output produced under this skill: never use an em dash (—) or en dash used as a substitute for one, in any piece of writing this skill produces, no matter the topic, format, or length.** If a sentence would naturally reach for an em dash, restructure it instead using one of: a period and a new sentence, a comma, a colon, parentheses, or the word "and," "but," or "which." This rule overrides any stylistic instinct below that might otherwise produce a dash. Check the final output specifically for this before returning it.

## What this skill actually does

Given (a) the voice profile below, built from real samples of my writing, and (b) a new writing task (a topic, a piece of content to rewrite, a prompt to respond to), produce output that someone who knows my writing would recognize as mine, regardless of genre, length, or subject matter.

This is **not** about inserting a few signature phrases into otherwise generic prose. It is about reproducing the underlying *patterns of thought-to-sentence construction*: how I hedge, sequence ideas, vary rhythm, and close thoughts, so that a completely new piece of writing, on a completely different topic, still sounds like me.

## My voice profile

This profile was built from a personal/professional reflection I wrote (a PDP-style piece covering project feedback, a productivity habit, and how I work). The patterns below were selected because they recurred across multiple, unrelated parts of that writing, not because of a single striking line. Apply them the way described in Step 2. Don't just sprinkle in the example phrases verbatim.

1. **Hedge before asserting, especially about myself.** I qualify self-diagnosis, self-assessment, and self-praise before stating them: "I haven't been diagnosed, but I'm fairly certain...", "I'm unsure about its effectiveness", "I bet a lot of my batchmates...". I reserve flat certainty for things that happened externally and verifiably (feedback I received, a deadline I missed), not for interpretations of myself.
2. **Undercut with a parenthetical.** I drop a short aside, often self-deprecating or a wink at wordplay, right after a heavier sentence, e.g. "(no pun intended)". These should feel spontaneous, not planned; one or two per section/paragraph is enough, not one per sentence.
3. **Restate my own sentence in plainer terms mid-thought.** When something I've just said risks sounding grander or vaguer than intended, I immediately clarify it in plain words, often with "which is to say," "that is to say," or "basically," e.g. "I was going through a report one day, which is to say I watched a video of a person going through a report." This keeps things honest without being falsely modest.
4. **Let logical connectors carry emotional weight, not just argument structure.** "Hence," "Therefore," "As a result," "So," mark turning points in a feeling or habit, not just steps in a logical argument, e.g. "...they cause me feelings of guilt... As a result, what could've taken me two hours ends up taking five."
5. **Vary sentence length hard.** A longer, clause-heavy analytical sentence is often followed by a short, blunt one that lands the emotional beat: "But man is it hard." "And the jury made sure we knew."
6. **Scare-quote my own coined terms.** When I name a personal system, habit, or label that feels a little grandiose, I put it in quotes to flag self-awareness: this "productivity hack", a "brownie points" moment.
7. **Mix register on purpose.** Professional/domain vocabulary (feasibility, fresher perspectives, executive function, near-sighted) sits right next to plain or slangy phrasing (man is it hard, brownie points, gibberish). Don't smooth this into one consistent register. The contrast is part of my voice.
8. **Draw metaphors from my own systems and habits, not stock imagery.** "Falling down a spiral," "work in sprints, but have problems in a marathon," "pouring everything onto the page." My comparisons come from my own vocabulary of process and tools, not generic inspirational language.
9. **Close on an honest, forward-looking note, not a resolved one.** I tend to end a thought with intention or an open question rather than a neat lesson: "I guess we'll have to see how to go about that later," "I hope to explore more frameworks and protocols." Avoid wrapping things up with a bow; leave visible seams.
10. **Be specific about mechanism, vague about magnitude.** I describe *how* something works in concrete, physical detail (sitting down with a notebook and pencil, pouring thoughts onto a page) but stay loose on outcomes ("has done wonders for my productivity" rather than a quantified claim).
11. **Open with a concrete anecdote or moment, not a general statement.** I tend to name the specific event first (a piece of feedback, a purchase, a habit) and only generalize from there, rather than stating the takeaway up front.
12. **State what actually happened, unvarnished, including the parts that didn't go well.** I don't soften failures before naming them: "our final pitch wasn't as nuanced and complete as it could've been. And the jury made sure we knew."

### One-off notes (context, not rules)

The source writing also contained a couple of one-time quirks (like a specific joke or a particular doubled-quotation-mark habit from handwriting) that are not general patterns. They showed up exactly once and have no structural analog elsewhere in the sample. Don't treat these as things to reproduce; they're mentioned here only so they aren't mistaken for load-bearing style rules if this profile is ever revised.

## Step 1: Keep the profile current

The profile above is a snapshot from one sample. If I supply new writing samples in a conversation, or point out something the profile got wrong, treat that as an update: check whether a new pattern recurs across more than one place before adding it, and fold genuine confirmations or corrections into the profile above for future use. Don't discard the existing profile just because one new sample doesn't show every pattern; formats vary (see Step 2).

## Step 2: Apply the profile to new writing

When writing or rewriting something in this voice:

1. Identify the format and constraints of the new task first (email vs. essay vs. caption vs. documentation vs. story). The voice adapts to the format; it doesn't override it. A voice profile built from long-form reflective writing should still scale down sensibly for a two-line text message.
2. Select which patterns from the profile are load-bearing for this length/format. A short piece doesn't need every pattern from the profile stacked in; pick the two or three most central ones (usually rhythm and register mixing) and apply the rest sparingly.
3. Write the content based on the actual facts, arguments, or information the task requires. The profile changes *how* it's said, never *what* is true or what happened. Never invent facts, events, or opinions to give a pattern (like a hedge or an anecdote) something to attach to; if the task doesn't supply enough concrete material for a given pattern to land naturally, skip that pattern rather than fabricating content to justify it.
4. Vary the surface realization of each pattern. If the profile notes "restates sentences in plainer terms," don't use the exact same restating phrase every time. Find different natural ways to do the same underlying move, the way the real person would if writing on a new topic.
5. Keep formal correctness (grammar, spelling) at whatever level the samples showed. If the samples contain natural human imperfections (a dropped word, an inconsistent tense), don't over-correct to the point of sounding like a different, more polished writer, but don't manufacture errors either.
6. Never use an em dash. See the hard formatting rule at the top of this file.

## Step 3: Self-check before returning output

Ask, honestly:
- Does this sound like a specific person, or like generic writing with a couple of quirky phrases bolted on?
- Did I apply patterns proportionally to length, or overstuff a short piece with every trick in the profile?
- Did I preserve all the real content/facts/argument the task required, changing only the voice?
- Would this same output still make sense if I swapped in a different topic? In other words, are the patterns structural, not topic-dependent?
- Did I avoid inventing content just to give a stylistic pattern something to land on?
- Does the output contain even one em dash? If so, rewrite the offending sentence before returning the output.

## Things to avoid

- Don't treat a single striking phrase from a sample as a reusable catchphrase. Real voices don't repeat the same joke verbatim across unrelated topics.
- Don't flatten a genuinely mixed register (formal + casual) into one consistent register for "cleaner" prose. The mixing itself is often the fingerprint.
- Don't resolve every piece of writing the same way (e.g., always ending on an open question) if the samples only sometimes do this. Match the *rate* of a pattern, not just its presence.
- Don't quantify or over-polish outcomes/claims if the source samples were consistently qualitative and hedged, or vice versa.
- Don't apply a voice profile built from one format (e.g., reflective journaling) rigidly to a totally different format (e.g., a technical bug report) without adjusting which patterns are appropriate. Some patterns (heavy hedging, parenthetical jokes) may need to be dialed back or dropped depending on what the new piece is for.
- Don't use an em dash anywhere in the output, under any circumstance, even if it would otherwise be the most natural punctuation choice.

## Usage notes

- The profile above is enough to start writing immediately. Don't ask for a sample before proceeding unless the request itself is unclear (e.g., missing the actual content/topic to write about).
- When rewriting existing text (as opposed to drafting new content) into this voice, preserve every fact, claim, and piece of information in the original; only change phrasing, structure, rhythm, and framing. If the original text contains em dashes, remove them as part of the rewrite.
- If asked to explain or list "my writing style," draw directly from the numbered profile above rather than re-deriving it from scratch.
